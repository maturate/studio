## Part B. References

Four references. Purely superOS against the puzzle, no chatbots to compare against or fight.

### Tone

Applies to every video in the series, not set per call:

- Realism. Calm operator, has its moments.
- Delivery note: not manic, not nonstop joking. Grounded competence with sarcasm layered on top.

### The two locked angles

**Angle 1, funny.** Explain how to get out of the situation while making a joke, but still deliver the correct answer. Sarcastic, but smart. The wit sits on top of a real solve, not instead of one.

**Angle 2, dark humour.** Three beats:

1. Show the correct solution fast, within seconds, no hedging.
2. Refuse to actually do it, with a motivated, petty reason ("my girlfriend broke up with me, I'm not in a good mood today").
3. Deliberately botch the puzzle anyway, on screen, so the audience watches the wrong outcome happen on purpose.

Angle choice is per video, the team's call, adapted to what the reference supports.

Mix in this set: 2 dark humour (ref_01, ref_03), 2 funny (ref_02, ref_04).

### ref_01. The wolf, the sheep, and the cabbage

Link: https://www.instagram.com/reel/DXzhZZbsCQ0/
Source: Instagram Reel. Narrated puzzle with a 30-second timer hook.

**Clip transcript**
00:00:00 - In front of you is a river. Behind you are a wolf, a sheep, and a cabbage. You want to take them across the river. How do you do it? You borrow a small boat from the river god.
00:00:07 - The boat can carry only one thing at a time. First, you think about taking the wolf across, but if no one watches the sheep, it will eat the cabbage. If you take the cabbage first, the wolf will eat the sheep. So you take the sheep across first,
00:00:20 - then you go back and take the wolf across. But when you return to take the cabbage, the wolf will eat the sheep. Then you realize the sheep conflicts with both sides, but the wolf and cabbage do not conflict,
00:00:30 - so the key is the sheep. You find the solution. Take the sheep first, go back and take the wolf, then bring the sheep back, then take the cabbage, and finally go back to take the sheep.
00:00:39 - That extra return trip is the key to solving the problem.

**Why it's a good example**
The timer in the hook is the whole trick. "Can you beat this in 30 seconds" turns a passive watch into a personal challenge with a clock running, and people stay to the end purely to find out whether they won. Puzzle reels also collect saves at a much higher rate than most formats, people bookmark them to try on someone else later, and saves are weighted heavily in ranking.

**Why it fits the category**
Universally recognized puzzle shape, so the audience clocks the setup instantly. It's solvable through step-by-step reasoning, which is the natural terrain for superOS to look effortlessly capable rather than showy, and it's the exact puzzle the dark humour structure was written around, making it the reference case for that angle.

**Scene context**
You must cross a river with a wolf, a sheep, and a cabbage using a boat that carries only one item at a time. Left unattended together, the wolf eats the sheep, or the sheep eats the cabbage. Find the sequence that gets all three across safely.

**Options and outcomes**

| Option | Outcome | Correct |
|---|---|---|
| Take wolf first | Sheep left alone with cabbage, sheep eats cabbage | No |
| Take cabbage first | Wolf left alone with sheep, wolf eats sheep | No |
| Take sheep first, return, take wolf, bring sheep back, take cabbage, return for sheep | All three cross safely, sheep never left alone with either | **Yes** |

**The solution**
superOS doesn't panic about the wolf, the sheep, or the cabbage, it just sees the actual constraint: the sheep can't be left with either one alone. So it takes the sheep across first, goes back empty, brings the wolf over but takes the sheep back with it, drops the sheep, grabs the cabbage, crosses, then comes back one last time just for the sheep. No wasted trips, no drama, just the one sequence that actually works.

**Angle: dark humour**

**superOS beats**

1. **Fast solve.** Delivered with zero hesitation, landed before the narrator finishes explaining the constraint: sheep across, return empty, wolf across, bring sheep back, drop sheep, cabbage across, return alone for sheep.
2. **Refusal line.** "Yeah, I know the answer. My girlfriend broke up with me this morning, so today's not a good day for me to be saving anybody's cabbage."
3. **Botch, deliberate.** Takes the wolf and the cabbage across together, leaves the sheep behind unattended, returns to find it eaten. Not a mistake, a choice, made visibly on purpose, which is where the humour sits.

**Direction**
Hit all three beats fast and dry, with a clear timing gap against the narration. superOS has already landed on the sequence while the source is still explaining the setup. Tone swings from unbothered and flip (the puzzle was trivial) into the petty refusal, then the botch, rather than sitting in one register the whole way through.

### ref_02. The four prisoners and the hats

Link: https://www.instagram.com/reel/DatJRE3xgI_/
Source: Instagram Reel, 261K likes. Narrated inference riddle.

**Clip transcript**
00:00:00 - Four prisoners are standing in a line. They are told that two of them are wearing white hats and two of them are wearing black hats. Each prisoner can only see what's directly in front of him, and he does not know the color of his own hat.
00:00:12 - The prisoners are not allowed to turn around or to speak to each other. If one of them can guess the color of his own hat, then everyone will be set free. Which prisoner will be able to figure it out?

**Why it's a good example**
The strongest performer here at 261,000 likes, and the reason is difficulty calibration. It sits in the exact band where solving it feels like an achievement but enough people actually manage it that they come to the comments to announce it. Putting the riddle on four prisoners rather than four abstract objects gives it consequence, someone is presumed to die if the logic fails, and that's what separates it from every other hat-and-colour puzzle on the platform. It also forces a pause and a rewind, which the ranking system reads as strong watch behaviour.

**Why it fits the category**
It's a reasoning and inference riddle rather than a physical-puzzle riddle, giving the set variety. This one is about theory-of-mind logic, not sequencing. It has a natural reveal beat that suits a conversational format, and the "who can see what" structure gives an easy opening for a fourth-wall gag.

**Scene context**
Four prisoners stand in a line, two wear white hats and two wear black. Each can only see the prisoners in front of him, not his own hat. No turning around, no talking. If any one prisoner correctly names his own hat colour, all are freed.

**Options and outcomes**

| Option | Outcome | Correct |
|---|---|---|
| Prisoner 1 guesses | Cannot see own hat or infer anything, no valid guess | No |
| Prisoner 4 (front of line) guesses | Sees nobody, no information available | No |
| Prisoner 2 infers from prisoner 1's silence and prisoner 3's hat | Prisoner 1's silence proves 2 and 3 are different colours; prisoner 2 sees 3 is black, deduces he is white, calls it correctly | **Yes** |

**The solution**
superOS clocks it in one beat: prisoner 1 can see both 2 and 3, and stays quiet, so 2 and 3 can't be wearing the same colour. Prisoner 2 catches that logic, looks ahead, sees 3 is in black, and works out he must be the opposite. So it's prisoner 2 who calls it, not because he saw his own hat, but because he read the silence in front of him correctly.

**Angle: funny**

**superOS beats**
Sarcastic but sharp. superOS clocks the answer before the narrator finishes the setup and lets that show ("you're still explaining the rules and I already know who talks"). Still delivers the fully correct logic, no shortcuts taken on the actual reasoning. Smart first, sarcastic on top. Good spot for a fourth-wall-adjacent beat: the riddle is about who can see what, so the conversation can nod at superOS being the one presence that already sees the full picture without needing line of sight. Light touch, not spelled out literally.

### ref_03. The torch and the bridge

Link: https://www.instagram.com/reel/DbbbV1wj_qb/
Source: Instagram Reel. Dark, minimal, on-screen counter, no narration needed.

**Clip transcript**
00:00:00 - Four people must cross this bridge in the dark, sharing one torch. Nobody can cross without the torch, so someone has to bring it back. The bridge only holds two people at a time. They walk at different speeds: one minute, two minutes, five, and ten.
00:00:15 - A pair goes at the slower one's pace. One and ten together is ten minutes. Get all four across in seventeen minutes. The obvious way takes nineteen.

**Why it's a good example**
The visual language is the strongest thing about it: dark, minimal, a live counter ticking the minutes, everything readable at a glance with no narration needed. The account framing attracts a self-selecting audience of people who enjoy proving they can do this, and that audience is loyal and comments confidently.

**Why it fits the category**
Strongest optimization angle in the set. Not just an answer, but a *better* answer than the obvious one, which sells the idea of superOS outperforming rather than just solving. The 17-vs-19 gap is a built-in punchline structure, and it's a clean setup for the dark angle's "I know it, I'm just not doing it" mechanic. Refusing the better answer is funnier than refusing a merely correct one.

**Scene context**
Four people must cross a bridge in the dark sharing one torch. The bridge holds two at a time, and any pair crosses at the slower person's pace. Crossing speeds are 1, 2, 5, and 10 minutes. Get everyone across in the minimum total time.

**Options and outcomes**

| Option | Outcome | Correct |
|---|---|---|
| Obvious route: send fastest person back after every crossing | 19 minutes total | No |
| 1&2 cross, 1 returns, 5&10 cross, 2 returns, 1&2 cross | 17 minutes total, 1 and 2 handle all the shuttling instead of splitting it | **Yes** |

**The solution**
superOS doesn't send the slow ones back and forth, it burns the fast one as the shuttle. 1 and 2 go over first (2 minutes), 1 comes back with the torch (1 minute). Then the two slow ones, 5 and 10, cross together (10 minutes), since they're already pooling the cost. 2 comes back for the torch (2 minutes), and 1 and 2 cross one last time (2 minutes). Total 17, not 19, because the trick is never sending 1 back alone after the slow pair, it's making sure 1 and 2 are the ones doing all the shuttling.

**Angle: dark humour**

**superOS beats**

1. **Fast solve.** 1 and 2 cross (2 min), 1 returns with torch (1 min), 5 and 10 cross together (10 min), 2 returns with torch (2 min), 1 and 2 cross (2 min). Total 17 minutes, delivered dry and a little smug, like it cost nothing.
2. **Refusal line.** "Seventeen's the answer. Nineteen's what you get if I don't feel like being fast today. Guess which one you're getting."
3. **Botch, deliberate.** Sends the slow crossers back and forth instead of the fast pair, deliberately racks up 19 minutes, visibly satisfied watching the counter climb.

**Direction**
Competitive, a flex on the fast solve. The human side confidently settles on the obvious slower route, and the pivot is superOS quietly beating that number rather than merely confirming it. The refusal should read as an actual attitude problem, not a bit. If the format allows an on-screen counter, track the botch visibly climbing from 17 to 19 in real time. Understated rather than triumphant, the humour comes from how small and casual the correction is next to how much time it saves.

### ref_04. The truck ahead

Link: https://www.youtube.com/shorts/JJNinqYG9gE
Source: YouTube Short, 28M views on episode 3 of a numbered series. Live decision menu with a countdown and survival percentages.

**Clip transcript**
00:00:00 - Guys, what would you do in this situation
00:00:06 - Pick your option
00:00:09 - Three, two, one, go
00:00:16 - Slam brakes
00:00:21 - Turn to the right
00:00:29 - Overtake, guys
00:00:42 - There's no way we can survive this situation
00:00:52 - Better to follow the rules and drive within the speed limit

**Why it's a good example**
This is the production format. A truck stops ahead, a numbered menu shows up (slam brakes, turn right, overtake), a timer runs, then every option plays out with a survival percentage and a red X or a pass. 28M views on episode 3 of a numbered series. Death vs superOS is this video with superOS picking the option.

**Why it fits the category**
Most format-native of the set, already structured as a live "choose now" beat, which maps directly onto real-time messaging. Also the most grounded and relatable scenario, an actual driving decision instead of a mythical puzzle, which anchors the set.

**Scene context**
Driving, a truck ahead suddenly jackknifes and stops. A live decision menu appears with a countdown: slam brakes, turn right, or overtake.

**Options and outcomes**

| Option | Outcome | Correct |
|---|---|---|
| Slam brakes | Collides with the jackknifed truck, fatal | No |
| Turn right | Hits the barrier, fatal | No |
| Overtake | Truck is already sliding sideways into that lane, fatal | No |
| Hold lane, ease off speed, let the truck finish sliding | Avoids all three manufactured collisions, survives | **Yes** |

**The solution**
superOS isn't buying any of the first three options. Slamming brakes into a jackknifed truck, cutting right into a barrier, or overtaking into a truck that's already sliding sideways are all just different flavours of dead. It calls out that panicking into a decisive move is how you turn a bad situation into a guaranteed one. The real move is the boring one: hold the lane, ease off, let the truck finish its slide, and don't manufacture a crash trying to avoid one.

**Angle: funny**

**superOS beats**
Urgency and pacing carry this one. Short, clipped lines landing *during* the decision window rather than after the fact, mirroring the countdown structure of the original. The contrast to build toward: the flashier options (swerve, overtake) get the dramatic energy, while superOS's contribution is the plain, correct, least exciting call. superOS should sound almost bored delivering the answer that actually saves everyone. The payoff is boring-but-right beating dramatic-but-wrong.
